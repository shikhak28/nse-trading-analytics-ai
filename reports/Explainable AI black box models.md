# Opening the black box: XAI's uneasy progress

Explainable AI (XAI) has produced a mature toolkit — LIME, SHAP, Grad-CAM, counterfactuals, Anchors, integrated gradients — for making opaque models legible, but the field's own most-cited recent work argues this entire toolkit rests on a shaky premise: post-hoc explanations of a black box are approximations that can look faithful while being provably manipulable, and Cynthia Rudin's 2019 *Nature Machine Intelligence* paper contends that for high-stakes structured-data decisions, the accuracy-interpretability tradeoff justifying black boxes in the first place is often a myth ([arXiv:1811.10154](https://opencasebook.org/casebooks/1439-ethics-and-governance-of-artificial-intelligence-fall-2020/resources/6.3-stop-explaining-black-box-machine-learning-models-for-high-stakes-decisions-and-use-interpretable-models-instead-by-cynthia-rudin-nature-2019/)). No formal, agreed-upon definition of "interpretability" exists even after a decade of research ([Lipton, arXiv:1606.03490](https://arxiv.org/abs/1606.03490); [Doshi-Velez & Kim, arXiv:1702.08608](https://arxiv.org/abs/1702.08608)), yet demand for explanations is now legally binding in specific contexts: US credit law (ECOA/Regulation B) has required "principal reasons" for credit denial since the 1970s ([Consumer Compliance Outlook](https://www.consumercomplianceoutlook.org/2021/fourth-issue/advanced-topics-in-adverse-action-notices-under-the-equal-credit-opportunity-act/)), and the EU AI Act's Article 13 transparency obligations for high-risk systems took effect in August 2024 ([artificialintelligenceact.eu](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/article/13/)). The result is a field caught between engineering maturity and conceptual crisis: dozens of well-engineered attribution methods exist, benchmarked across six-plus quantitative dimensions by tools like Quantus and OpenXAI, yet the deepest papers in the space — Rudin (2019), Lakkaraju & Bastani (2020), Slack et al. (2020), Ghorbani et al. (2019) — collectively show that faithfulness, the property an explanation most needs to have, is neither guaranteed nor easily verified, and that explanations can be adversarially weaponized to hide bias rather than reveal it. This report traces that arc: what interpretability actually means, how the technique landscape is organized, what the evaluation science says about explanation quality, where XAI is legally and clinically consequential today, and why the field's frontier — LLM mechanistic interpretability — is running into the same faithfulness problem at a more dangerous scale.

## Interpretability has no agreed definition, but a working hierarchy exists

Despite XAI's decade of growth, foundational papers repeatedly concede there is "little consensus on what interpretable machine learning is and how it should be measured" ([Doshi-Velez & Kim, arXiv:1702.08608](https://arxiv.org/abs/1702.08608)). Zachary Lipton's widely cited critique goes further, arguing interpretability is "not a monolithic concept but a cluster of frequently conflated desiderata," used incompatibly across papers ([Lipton, arXiv:1606.03490](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1606.03490)). Out of this ambiguity a rough working hierarchy has emerged: **transparency** — literally understanding a model's internal mechanism, which Lipton further splits into *simulatability* (a person can mentally run the whole model), *decomposability* (each part has an intuitive meaning), and *algorithmic transparency* (understanding the learning procedure's guarantees) — sits at the base; **interpretability**, per Christoph Molnar, is "the degree to which a human can understand the cause of a decision"; and **explainability** is "a stronger term requiring interpretability and additional context," typically meaning a generated, human-facing justification rather than raw mechanistic insight ([Molnar, Interpretable Machine Learning](https://christophm.github.io/interpretable-ml-book/interpretability.html)). Philosopher Tim Miller supplies the missing human half of this picture, defining an explanation formally as "an answer to a why-question" with an *explanandum* (what needs explaining) and an *explanans* (the answer), and crucially distinguishing a true explanation of the decision process from a mere *justification* that "explains why a decision is good" without describing how it was actually reached ([Miller, arXiv:1706.07269](https://arxiv.org/abs/1706.07269)) — a distinction that becomes central later when post-hoc methods are shown to generate plausible-looking justifications rather than faithful explanations.

Demand for XAI clusters around five motivations that Lipton first systematized: **trust** that a deployed model hasn't learned spurious shortcuts, **causality** (using the model to generate testable real-world hypotheses), **transferability** to non-stationary or adversarial conditions, **informativeness** beyond a bare prediction, and **fair/ethical decision-making**, explicitly including "the right to explanation" ([Lipton, arXiv:1606.03490](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1606.03490)). Concrete triggers repeatedly cited in the literature include the COMPAS recidivism tool, criticized for using race and gender-correlated proxies in bail/sentencing risk scores ([summarized via Posos](https://www.posos.co/blog-articles/explainable-ai-part-1-understanding-how-ai-makes-decisions)), and GDPR's Article 22, which gives data subjects a right against decisions "based solely on automated processing" with "legal or significant effects," plus rights to human intervention, to express a viewpoint, and to contest the decision ([GDPR Article 22](https://gdpr-text.com/read/article-22/); [ICO guidance](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/uk-gdpr-guidance-and-resources/individual-rights/individual-rights/rights-related-to-automated-decision-making-including-profiling/)). Notably, no primary source in this research identifies a specific named autonomous-vehicle accident as a documented trigger for XAI policy — the AV safety motivation in the literature is generic (Doshi-Velez & Kim cite "semi-autonomous vehicles" as a safety-verification domain), not tied to one incident, and should not be overstated.

## Four orthogonal axes classify every XAI method

Across Molnar's book, Adadi & Berrada's 2018 IEEE Access survey (4,300+ citations per secondary aggregation), and Arrieta et al.'s 2020 Information Fusion survey, the same four independent classification axes recur: **stage** (intrinsic/ante-hoc models that are interpretable by design, like linear regression, decision trees, and GAMs, versus post-hoc methods applied after training to an already-opaque model); **scope** (local, explaining one prediction, versus global, explaining overall model behavior); **specificity** (model-agnostic methods that only need input-output access versus model-specific methods that exploit internal structure like gradients or tree splits); and **output format** (feature attribution, rule/decision-set, example-based/counterfactual, or visualization) ([Molnar ToC](https://christophm.github.io/interpretable-ml-book/interpretability.html); [Arrieta et al. via Sogeti Labs synthesis](https://labs.sogeti.com/explainable-artificial-intelligence-xai-general-taxonomy/)). These axes combine freely — LIME is post-hoc + model-agnostic + local + feature-attribution, while Grad-CAM is post-hoc + model-specific + local + visualization — and mapping any new method against all four dimensions is the fastest way to understand where it sits in the landscape. Sorting the twelve techniques covered in this report against local/global and agnostic/specific produces a clear pattern: the **model-agnostic + local quadrant is by far the most crowded** (LIME, Anchors, counterfactual explanations, KernelSHAP), suggesting this is where the least methodological consensus exists, while the model-specific/global quadrant contains essentially one entry (Gini/impurity importance, restricted to tree ensembles).

Mechanistically, LIME perturbs an instance's inputs, queries the black box, and fits a sparse local linear surrogate weighted by proximity ([arXiv:1602.04938](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1602.04938)). SHAP generalizes this by grounding attribution in **Shapley values** from cooperative game theory — each feature's average marginal contribution across all possible orderings of "adding" features to a baseline — and proves this is the *unique* solution among additive attribution methods satisfying local accuracy, missingness, and consistency, guarantees LIME lacks ([Lundberg & Lee, NeurIPS PDF](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper/7062-a-unified-approach-to-interpreting-model-predictions.pdf)). Exact Shapley computation is NP-hard, so practical SHAP relies on approximations: KernelSHAP (model-agnostic, sampling-based, still exponential in the worst case) and TreeSHAP (exact, polynomial-time, but tree-ensembles only) ([arXiv:2502.12295](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2502.12295); [Fast TreeSHAP, arXiv:2109.09847](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2109.09847)). Gradient-based methods form their own family: saliency maps take the raw gradient of a class score with respect to input pixels ([Simonyan et al., arXiv:1312.6034](https://arxiv.org/abs/1312.6034v2)); Grad-CAM globally-average-pools gradients flowing into the last convolutional layer to weight feature maps into a coarse heatmap, requiring no architecture changes and working across CNN-family models ([Selvaraju et al., arXiv:1610.02391](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1610.02391)); and Integrated Gradients fixes the saturation problem of single-point gradients by integrating gradients along a path from a baseline to the actual input, satisfying formal Sensitivity and Implementation Invariance axioms ([Sundararajan, Taly & Yan, ICML 2017](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v70/sundararajan17a/sundararajan17a.pdf)). Counterfactual explanations sidestep model internals entirely, instead solving an optimization problem for the smallest input change that flips the outcome — e.g., "had your income been $5,000 higher, your loan would have been approved" ([Wachter, Mittelstadt & Russell, arXiv:1711.00399](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1711.00399)) — while Anchors search for IF-THEN rules that "anchor" a prediction with a PAC-style precision guarantee, and were shown in the original user study to let people predict model behavior more accurately than LIME-style explanations ([Ribeiro et al., AAAI 2018](https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/AAAI/article/view/11491)). Attention visualization occupies a genuinely contested position: Jain & Wallace (2019) showed attention weights correlate poorly with gradient-based importance and that very different attention patterns can produce identical predictions, undermining their status as explanations, while Wiegreffe & Pinter's rebuttal argues attention retains signal under the right analysis — this remains an open dispute rather than settled fact ([arXiv:1902.10186](https://arxiv.org/abs/1902.10186); [arXiv:1908.04626](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1908.04626)). Rounding out the global-model tools, PDPs average a model's prediction over a feature's marginal distribution but can mask heterogeneous effects that cancel out, which is precisely why ICE plots decompose the PDP into one line per instance ([Molnar's IML book, ICE chapter](https://christophm.github.io/interpretable-ml-book/ice.html)), and permutation importance measures the accuracy drop from shuffling a feature but is known to understate importance for correlated features, while Gini/impurity importance is fast but biased toward high-cardinality features ([scikit-learn docs](https://scikit-learn.org/stable/modules/permutation_importance.html); [explained.ai](https://explained.ai/rf-importance/)). Nearly every method has a well-documented Achilles' heel: LIME's explanations vary 16%–28.5% across repeated runs due to sampling randomness (spawning fixes like BayLIME, S-LIME, and OptiLIME) ([BayLIME, arXiv:2012.03058](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2012.03058)), and Ghorbani et al. showed imperceptible adversarial perturbations can drastically shift saliency, DeepLIFT, and Integrated Gradients maps while leaving the predicted label untouched ([arXiv:1710.10547](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1710.10547)).

## Explanation quality has no ground truth, only competing proxies

The hardest problem in evaluating XAI is structural: unlike a classification task with known labels, an explanation is a claim about *why* a model acted, and the model's true internal reasoning is exactly what the explanation method was needed to surface in the first place — there is no independent ground truth to check against ([Doshi-Velez & Kim, arXiv:1702.08608](https://arxiv.org/abs/1702.08608)). The field has responded by triangulating multiple proxy dimensions rather than converging on one metric. Quantus (Hedström et al., JMLR 2023) organizes 35+ quantitative metrics into six categories — **faithfulness**, **robustness**, **complexity**, **localisation**, **randomisation**, and **axiomatic properties** ([JMLR paper](http://jmlr.org/papers/v24/22-0142.html)) — while OpenXAI (Agarwal et al., NeurIPS 2022) provides 22 metrics and the first public XAI leaderboards, organized around faithfulness, stability, and fairness ([NeurIPS proceedings](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2022/hash/65398a0eba88c9b4a1c38ae405b125ef-Abstract-Datasets_and_Benchmarks.html)). Concretely, fidelity is usually tested by an "erase-and-observe" logic that recurs across both vision and NLP: Petsiuk et al.'s Deletion/Insertion metrics remove or reveal pixels in claimed-importance order and track the resulting drop/rise in predicted probability ([arXiv:2201.13291](https://arxiv.org/abs/2201.13291)), while ERASER's **comprehensiveness** (confidence drop when the rationale is removed) and **sufficiency** (confidence retained when only the rationale is kept) formalize the same idea for NLP rationales ([DeYoung et al., arXiv:1911.03429](https://arxiv.org/abs/1911.03429)).

Two landmark studies established that this fidelity signal can be dangerously incomplete. Alvarez-Melis & Jaakkola (2018) showed that similar inputs frequently produce dissimilar explanations from popular methods, formalizing **robustness** as a distinct desideratum that current methods largely fail ([arXiv:1806.08049](https://arxiv.org/abs/1806.08049)), and Ghorbani et al. (2019) showed neural network interpretations are "fragile" — small, potentially adversarial perturbations can dramatically alter an explanation while the model's prediction and true behavior stay fixed, a finding the authors flag as especially dangerous in medicine, where a saliency map might be mistaken for causal insight ([AAAI 2019](https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/AAAI/article/view/4252)). Beyond automated metrics, Doshi-Velez & Kim's three-level evaluation taxonomy — **application-grounded** (real experts, real task, expensive but most convincing), **human-grounded** (simplified tasks with lay subjects), and **functionally-grounded** (no humans, proxy metrics like sparsity) — functions as a cost/rigor ladder, and it's telling that nearly every automated benchmark discussed above (Quantus, OpenXAI, ERASER) sits only at the cheapest, functionally-grounded level; genuine application-grounded studies with domain experts remain rare in the literature surveyed here.

## Explanations are legally required, clinically consequential, and sometimes weaponizable

XAI's real-world stakes are clearest where law already forces the issue. In US lending, **ECOA and Regulation B** have required creditors to give specific, non-generic "principal reasons" for denying credit since long before modern ML, and the CFPB has published direct guidance on producing legally compliant adverse-action notices from AI/ML underwriting models ([CFPB Innovation Spotlight](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/about-us/blog/innovation-spotlight-providing-adverse-action-notices-when-using-ai-ml-models/)); SHAP-based Shapley decomposition has become the standard technique for generating these reason codes, formalized in "Explaining Adverse Actions in Credit Decisions Using Shapley Decomposition" ([arXiv:2204.12365](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2204.12365)) and applied at scale to 15,000 SME borrowers in peer-to-peer lending ([Bussmann et al., Computational Economics](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10614-020-10042-0)). Yet a documented compliance critique cuts against relying purely on post-hoc tools: "the model that issues the decision should be the model that produces the reasons... SHAP on a black box gives you a story while an interpretable model gives you the actual driver" ([Witness AI](https://witness.ai/blog/explainable-ai-in-finance/)), and a 2025 case study found SHAP explanations can shift meaningfully across model recalibrations, threatening the legal defensibility of reason codes that must stay consistent over time ([arXiv:2508.01851](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2508.01851)). In healthcare, the canonical cautionary tale is Caruana et al.'s 2015 pneumonia-risk study: an intelligible generalized additive model caught the spurious learned rule that "having asthma lowers risk of dying from pneumonia" — an artifact of asthmatics historically receiving more aggressive care — a rule a deployed black-box neural net would have encoded silently, potentially triaging a high-risk group as low-risk ([Caruana et al., KDD 2015](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2017/06/KDD2015FinalDraftIntelligibleModels4HealthCare_igt143e-caruanaA.pdf)). In imaging diagnosis, Grad-CAM/SHAP hybrids are now standard for diabetic retinopathy screening, with one hybrid CNN-Transformer model's clinician-rated Clinical Relevance Score rising from **4.0/5 to 4.8/5** after adding Grad-CAM visualizations ([Zenodo](https://zenodo.org/records/21387368)) — though the imaging-XAI literature itself cautions that visual saliency "does not necessarily equal causal significance," so these heatmaps should not be read as proof of correct model reasoning ([PMC](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12649958/)). Separately, Tonekaboni et al. (2019) surveyed ICU and ED clinicians and found that generic SHAP-style feature-attribution output is often not what clinicians actually need to trust and act on a model — explainability requirements are context-specific, not one-size-fits-all ([arXiv:1905.05134](https://arxiv.org/abs/1905.05134)). In autonomous vehicles, research has moved faster than binding rules: Kim & Canny's causal-attention visualization for steering models and the BDD-X dataset for natural-language driving justifications are established research directions ([arXiv:1703.10631](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1703.10631); [ECCV 2018](https://openaccess.thecvf.com/content_ECCV_2018/papers/Jinkyu_Kim_Textual_Explanations_for_ECCV_2018_paper.pdf)), while actual regulatory activity (UNECE's GRVA working party) focuses on post-incident forensic recorders (EDR/DSSAD, an aviation-style "black box") rather than real-time explanation ([Council of Europe](https://assembly.coe.int/LifeRay/JUR/Pdf/DocsAndDecs/2020/AS-JUR-2020-20-EN.pdf)).

At the policy level, GDPR's "right to explanation" remains contested rather than settled: Goodman & Flaxman (2016) argued Article 22 combined with Articles 13–15 creates such a right, while Wachter, Mittelstadt & Floridi (2017) rebutted that GDPR mandates only a narrower ex-ante "right to be informed," not a full ex-post explanation of a specific decision ([arXiv:1606.08813](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1606.08813); [Wachter et al., IDPL 2017](https://academic.oup.com/idpl/article/7/2/76/3860948)). The **EU AI Act** (Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, in force since August 2024) has since moved past this ambiguity with binding text: Article 13 requires high-risk AI systems to be "designed and developed to ensure that their operation is sufficiently transparent to enable deployers to interpret a system's output," with most obligations phasing in from August 2026 ([artificialintelligenceact.eu](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/article/13/)). The US, by contrast, remains a patchwork: NIST's voluntary AI RMF names "explainable and interpretable" as one of seven trustworthy-AI characteristics but creates no binding obligation ([NIST AI RMF](https://www.nist.gov/publications/artificial-intelligence-risk-management-framework-ai-rmf-10)), the FTC relies on general Section 5 unfair/deceptive-practices authority rather than an AI-specific mandate ([FTC guidance summary](https://wp.nyu.edu/compliance_enforcement/2021/05/10/the-future-of-ai-regulation-the-ftcs-new-guidance-on-using-ai-truthfully-fairly-and-equitably/)), and binding teeth instead show up state-by-state, as in Colorado's AI Act requiring a plain-language explanation within 30 days of an adverse consequential decision plus a right to request human review ([Skadden](https://www.skadden.com/insights/publications/2024/06/colorados-landmark-ai-act)).

## The field's frontier problem is that explanations can lie

The most consequential open problem in XAI is not a missing technique but a structural weakness in the ones that already exist: **faithfulness is not guaranteed and is provably breakable**. Rudin's 2019 *Nature Machine Intelligence* paper argues post-hoc explanations "are not reliable, and can be misleading" by construction, since an explanation model is a simpler function approximating the black box and can disagree with it on many inputs while looking accurate on average — and contends that for high-stakes structured-data decisions (credit, criminal justice, healthcare risk), interpretable-by-design models can often match black-box accuracy, making the entire post-hoc explanation enterprise for such domains a bad practice that should stop ([arXiv:1811.10154](https://opencasebook.org/casebooks/1439-ethics-and-governance-of-artificial-intelligence-fall-2020/resources/6.3-stop-explaining-black-box-machine-learning-models-for-high-stakes-decisions-and-use-interpretable-models-instead-by-cynthia-rudin-nature-2019/)). This is not a hypothetical concern: Lakkaraju & Bastani (2020) showed that an explanation with *perfect fidelity* to a black box can still conceal its biases, and in a user study with law/criminal-justice experts, misleading-but-fidelity-preserving explanations increased trust in a biased classifier by up to **9.8x** ([arXiv:1911.06473](https://arxiv.org/abs/1911.06473)). Slack et al. (2020) went further, demonstrating a "scaffolding" attack that lets an adversary construct a classifier whose LIME/SHAP explanations look innocuous while its real predictions stay biased — validated on COMPAS-style data, showing racist decision rules can be hidden from explanation-based audits entirely ([arXiv:1911.02508](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1911.02508)). Two distinct attack surfaces are now documented: a malicious *model developer* can scaffold the model itself (Slack et al.), or a malicious *input* can exploit a fixed model's fragility (Ghorbani et al.) — both undermine using explanations as an audit mechanism, and no field-standard defense against either exists yet.

This faithfulness crisis is recurring at a more dangerous scale in large language models. Chain-of-thought (CoT) reasoning — the verbalized steps an LLM produces before an answer — is "frequently hypothesized" but not verified to reflect the model's actual computation, and 2025 work shows CoT explanations "in the wild" are "not always faithful," making them "imprecise, fragile, and misleading" in real deployment settings, not just adversarial constructions ([arXiv:2503.08679](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.08679v4)). This matters more for LLMs than for earlier black boxes because CoT is increasingly used as a safety and monitoring signal in reasoning models — if the verbalized reasoning doesn't track the real computation, that monitoring signal can fail silently. The leading rigorous alternative is **mechanistic interpretability**: Anthropic's sparse-autoencoder "dictionary learning" work decomposed a one-layer transformer's MLP into 4,000+ largely monosemantic features in 2023 and scaled the same technique to a production model, Claude 3 Sonnet, in 2024 ([Towards Monosemanticity](https://transformer-circuits.pub/2023/monosemantic-features); [Scaling Monosemanticity](https://transformer-circuits.pub/2024/scaling-monosemanticity/)). But a 29-author cross-lab review, "Open Problems in Mechanistic Interpretability" (Jan 2025), identifies **superposition** (individual neurons encoding many unrelated concepts) and **scalability** — turning feature-level understanding into whole-model understanding — as unresolved bottlenecks ([arXiv:2501.16496](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.16496)). Compounding all of this is a human-factors finding that closes the loop uncomfortably: a 2025 review found explanations can "inadvertently reinforce misplaced trust" regardless of their actual correctness, especially among less-expert users ([Romeo & Conti, AI & Society 2025](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1007/s00146-025-02422-7)), and a 2025 cybersecurity study found automation bias affected 47% of analysts trusting AI tools ([ACM Digital Threats](https://dl.acm.org/doi/full/10.1145/3759260)) — meaning the presence of an explanation, faithful or not, tends to raise human trust anyway, which is precisely the mechanism Lakkaraju & Bastani's 9.8x trust increase exploited.

## Conclusion

The throughline across definitions, techniques, evaluation science, and regulation is a widening gap between what XAI methods are *asked* to do (certify trustworthy AI for regulators, clinicians, and loan applicants) and what the strongest evidence says they can *actually* guarantee: post-hoc explanations are approximations, not verified accounts, and approximations can be gamed. This reframes the field's central future-direction debate — Rudin's "stop explaining, start building interpretable" argument versus the industry default of explaining whatever black box already exists — as less a stylistic preference and more a risk-management choice: interpretable-by-design models close the faithfulness gap entirely for structured/tabular high-stakes decisions, while post-hoc methods remain the only option for perceptual domains (vision, raw text, LLMs) where deep black boxes still dominate accuracy, and there the faithfulness problem is simply inherited rather than solved. The clearest emerging institutional risk is the "law-XAI translation gap" ([arXiv:2608.02699](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.02699)): regulators like the EU AI Act want technically validated explainability to reference in binding standards, researchers want stable regulatory specs before committing to architectures, and neither side can move first — meanwhile the human-factors evidence suggests that even a technically imperfect explanation reliably increases human trust, which is exactly the dynamic that makes an unresolved faithfulness problem dangerous rather than merely academic.
